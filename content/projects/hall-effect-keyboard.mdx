## The problem

A mechanical switch is a binary sensor: pressed or not. A Hall-effect switch
reports a magnetic field strength that varies continuously with travel — which
means the keyboard knows *how far down* each key is, and actuation depth
becomes a per-key setting rather than a property of the switch you soldered in.

Getting that requires a board that can read an analog value from every key,
cleanly, fast enough that no one would perceive latency. This project is that
board: the schematic, layout, and case, designed to be fabricated.

## Approach

**Getting 80+ analog signals into one microcontroller.** An STM32 doesn't have
an ADC channel per key, so the Hall sensors feed banks of analog multiplexers.
The STM32 drives the mux select lines and samples each mux output on an ADC
input, so the whole board can be scanned key by key. The ADC routing from each
mux to the microcontroller was laid out as its own concern, short and away from
anything switching.

**Analog integrity first.** The signal being measured is a small voltage
varying with magnet distance, on a board that also carries addressable RGB
switching current at every key. Getting a clean reading is a layout problem
before it is ever a firmware problem — ground pours, return paths, and keeping the
analog sense lines away from everything noisy.

**Separate analog and digital power.** Per-key RGB plus an OLED plus an STM32
doing continuous ADC sampling adds up, and LED switching noise on the sensor
supply shows up directly as jitter in the readings. The analog and digital
rails are distributed separately, with ferrite beads isolating them and
decoupling at every IC. Designing the power tree properly at the schematic
stage is much cheaper than discovering brownouts on assembled hardware.

**Designed to be debugged and plugged in.** USB for the host connection, an SWD
header for programming and on-chip debugging, an I2C header for the OLED, and
ESD protection on the lines a user's hands and cables actually touch.

**Manufacturability as a constraint, not an afterthought.** Component choices,
footprint decisions, and assembly tolerances were made against what can
actually be fabricated and hand-assembled — a board that only works in CAD is
not a board.

**Case designed alongside the PCB.** Mounting points, port cutouts, and the
plate were modelled in SolidWorks against the same board outline, so board
revisions and case revisions stayed in step.

## What I got out of it

A complete board design, and a much better feel for the parts of embedded
work that have nothing to do with code. Firmware bugs you can iterate on in
seconds; a board respin costs weeks. That asymmetry changes how carefully you
read a datasheet.
